**Government**

The government will enact fiscal policy. It is a set of financial strategy that influences the government spending and earnings. Government spending influences the infrastructure of the country and subsequently the employment rate of the nation, while taxation affects how much the average household and business can save or spend. 

During an economic downturn, the government will:
1. Increase their spending
    - This is to stimulate economic growth with public investments
    - As new infrastructures are built, the manpower needed to keep sustain such developments are needed.
        - This, as a result, leads to greater employment rate.
    - Additionally, as new and improved infrastructures and systems are built, it can incentivise the population to spend money or pay their taxes as they believe that their money is being put to good use. 
2. Decrease in taxes
    - The reduction in taxes allows households and businesses to have higher disposable income.
    - This gives them more flexibility to spend or save their money.
    - As a result, the tendency to spend their money increases, leading to higher overall amount of money in circulation, boosting the economy.

### Question A

1. Improved manufacturing productivity
    - This is due to the inventions of labour-saving technology.
    - With these inventions, the amount of manpower needed during the manufacturing process will decrease.
    - This leads to a lower employment rate in roles involving physical manufacturing.
    - Forcing people to look at other sectors, roles, or fields.
2. Wealthier population
    - As society progresses, so does the wealth of the population.
    - This allows the government to invest in fields outside of manufacturing, exposing people to a wide variety of sectors such as programming, accounting, law, and logistics.
    - These fields have higher barrier to entry and higher upfront costs.
    - The government's investments into education also serves as a catalyst that drives people to various fields.
3. Shift in the spending habits of the population
    - As the population gets wealthier, so does the spending habits of the people.
    - While there is a limit to how much physical good a person can have, there is no limit to how much experience and service that a person can demand.
    - Services are commonly known as superior goods as the demand for it grows faster than income.
    - In countries such as Malaysia, a middle income country may invest their profits to their children's education rather than buying more furnitures.
4. Countries moving their factories
    - Due to global trade barriers falling, companies are now building their factories in other countries with lower labour costs.
    - Western and East Asian companies are building their factories in South East Asian hubs such as Vietnam or Indonesia as their minimum wages is lower and they have less stringent rules.
    - Multinational companies keep the high-value services such as marketing, programming, and designing in their home country while they de-industrialise by moving their factories to other, less-developed countries. 
5. Outsourcing of in-house services
    - De-industrialisation may not necessarily be about reduction in manufacturing efforts, but rather about reclassification of jobs.
    - Initially, a large factory may have its own team of experts - accountants, programmers, and logistic experts.
    - However, in today's era, these factories are outsourcing the roles needed from companies that handle specialised services.
    - For example, a car manufacturer company, may hire external logistic experts. These experts are still considered service workers and are not under manufacturing sector.

### Question B

1. Competition for arable land and urbanisation
    - Rapid urbanisation and expansion of estate area are leading the depletion of high-quality, fertile land.
    - This leaves farmers with low-quality, marginal land.
    - Additionally, farmers are forced to work with small, scattered land.
        - This makes it more difficult for them to build the proper infrastructure necessary for efficient farming.
        - This also makes it more expensive for government officials to intervene.
2. Aging farmer population and labour shortage
    - As the youth leaves for urban industrial sectors, the volume of youths working in agriculture is rapidly decreasing.
    - This makes the average age of a farmer to be above 50 years old.
    - The youths view agriculture as dirty, dangerous, and difficult, offering low prestige with unstable pay unlike the roles in the industrial sectors.
    - This makes it more difficult for the agriculture sector to evolve as the youths needed to drive initiative is lacking.
3. Slow technological adoption
    - The cost needed to purchase and integrate modern technology like automatic irrigation, robotics, and drones into farms is expensive.
    - The high upfront cost in modernising a farm requires money that most small-scale farmers that make up the majority of the agriculture sector do not have.
    - This makes it difficult for these farmers to make their process more efficient or increase their output.
    - This keeps them uncompetitive from cheaper imports like those from China or the US.
4. Climate volatility and loss
    - The increasingly volatile climate has had substantial effect on Malaysia's crop calendar.
    - Devastating floods had wiped out hectare of crops in Penang and Johor.
    - Unlike the industrial sector, the agriculture sector does not have an insurance framework that protects farmers from total loss.
    - When a disaster hit, the entire possible income that a farmer can have is wiped out.
    - This forces farmers to keep farming low-value safe crops rather than experimenting with other crops that may bring more profit.
5. Fragmented value-chain and dependence on middleman
    - Farmers lack access to direct consumers and are forced to rely on a middlemen.
    - Most of the profit also goes to the middleman, leaving the farmers with little while having to deal with high post-harvest losses.
    - Due to the under-developed transportation infrastructure in rural areas, most farmers lose their profits from perishable goods such as fruits and vegetables.
    - The low volume also prevents farmers from direct negotiation with supermarkets.
