## Question 1

### Question A ⭐
1. Increase in manufacturing productivity
  - This can be attributed to labour-saving inventions such as machines that can automate tasks that previously require human labour
    - For example, in food production, packaging are done with machines rather than relying on manual efforts from humans
  - This then reduce the number of people employed in the manufacturing side of the manufacturing field
  - This incentives people to venture outside of manufacturing and into services. 
2. Increase in overall population wealth
  - As the country becomes wealthier, more money is invested into other sectors in the country. 
  - This exposes people to other sectors and fields such as programming, medicine, and engineering.
  - As the overall wealth increases, allows more people to venture into other sectors and fields like programming, medicine, and engineering
  - Additionally, investments into the education of the population serves as a catalyst that drove more people to pursue careers outside of manufacturing. 
  - This reduces the manpower going into manufacturing, forcing companies and governments to come up with creative solutions to sustain the manufacturing sector in Malaysia.
3. Globalisation and Offshoring
  - As global trade barriers fall, companies in developed or developing countries like Malaysia often move their manufacturing plants to countries with lower labour costs.
  - Multinational companies may find it cheaper to produce goods in countries like Vietnam or Indonesia due to lower minimum wages and less stringent regulations.
  - This leads to a shift where the home country keeps the high-value services such as design, marketing, and headquarters, but de-industrialise by closing down local physical factories.
  - Many Western and East Asian firms have moved their mass-production assembly lines out of their home countries to Southeast Asian hubs to maintain global price competitiveness.
    - It provides them with a much wider profit margin.
  - For example, Nike, a global sports brand, manufacture most of their products in Vietnam, allowing them to earn more from selling while keeping manufacturing costs low.
4. Changing Patterns of Consumer Demand
  - As society progresses, the way in which people spend their money changes. 
  - Once people are able to suffice their basic needs, their extra income is spent on services such as tourism, healthcare, luxury dining. Services are known as superior goods because the demand for them grows faster than income.
  - There is a limit to how many physical products a person needs, but the demand for experiences and specialised services is almost limitless.
  - For example, in a middle-income country like Malaysia, as households get richer, they spend more on private education and international travel than on buying more household furniture, causing the service sector to grow while manufacturing stays flat.
5. Outsourcing of in-house services
  - De-industrialisation can also be about how jobs are redefined rather than factories disappearing.
  - Initially, a large factory would have its own internal team of accountants, security guards, and cleaners.
    - Despite not being strictly manufacturing, these are still considered manufacturing.
  - Today, firms outsource these tasks to specialised service companies. The factory still produces the same amount of goods, but hundreds of jobs have moved from the "Manufacturing" sector to the "Service" sector.
  - For example, if a car manufacturer hires an external logistics firm to handle its shipping, the drivers are now "service workers" even though they are still part of the same industrial process.

1. Government
  - Expansionary Fiscal Policy
    - This means that the government spends more than the government earns
    - Government spending affects infrastructure growth and employment in Malaysia.
    - Taxation affects how much money households and businesses can save or spend
  - In this policy, the government increase their spending
    - The government stimulate growth with public investments
    - By spending more money in improving or creating infrastructure and systems, the employment rate in the country can increase.
    - This then increase the amount of money in circulation in the economy
  - The government also decrease taxation on the population and businesses
    - This increases the disposable income that the average households and businesses can have
    - Allowing people to expand their businesses, invest, and grow. 
2. Bank Negara Malaysia
  - Expansionary Monetary Policy
    - Set of financial strategies that is utilised by the central government during economic downturn
    - It is used to manage the overall money supply, influence interest rate, and control liquidity
    - The main goals are to create sustainable economic growth, manage inflation or deflation, and maintain price stability
  - The central bank will:
    1. Open Market Operations
      - The central bank will buy government securities from commercial banks
      - This increases the amount of money in reserve that each commercial bank has
      - This gives them more flexibility to loan money out to customers and businesses.
    2. Lowering of loan interest rate
      - By lowering the loan interest rate, businesses and individuals are more incentivise to loan money from the bank to get the capital needed to start or expand their businesses.
      - This is also influenced by the Overnight Policy Rate (OPR), benchmark for overnight lending between banks at night
    3. Lowering of Required Reserve Rate (RRR)
      - The RRR is the minimum deposit that each commercial bank must have that cannot be loaned out.
      - Lowering the RRR means that commercial banks have more money to loan to businesses and individuals
  - Overall these actions are done to improve liquidity, increase money in circulation, and improving growth in the country.

## Question 2

### Question A 

1.Aging Farmer Population and Labour Shortage
  - The agriculture sector is suffering labour drainage of youths, whereby youths are moving towards urban industrial sectors, leaving the average age of Malaysian farmers above 50 years old.
  - This creates a lack of human capital to drive modern agriculture initiatives, as older farmers are more likely to be resistant to digital changes.
  - Younger generations perceive agriculture as a dirty, dangerous, and difficult industry; it offers low prestige and unstable income compared to manufacturing or services. This results in an internal brain drain where most tech-savvy talent leaves rural areas.
  - Consequently, the sector suffers from a lack of innovation at the farm level, as young, talented workforce to manage drones, IoT sensors, or data analytics is lacking. 
  - This forces the industry to remain stuck in labour-intensive, low-yield traditional practices.
2. High Capital Costs and Slow Technology Adoption
  - Smallholders face a massive financial gap in adopting "smart farming" technologies like automated irrigation, robotics, and precision sensors.
  - The high cost makes production non-conductive for small-scale farmers who make up the majority of the food sector.
  - Modernising a farm requires significant upfront investment, which is often out of reach for small farmers. While government support systems exist, they are frequently criticised for being reactive rather than proactive. 
  - Because these farmers cannot afford the technology to increase their efficiency, they remain uncompetitive against cheaper imports from neighbouring countries like Thailand or Vietnam.
3. Competition for Arable Land and Urban Encroachment
  - Rapid urbanisation and the expansion of industrial estates are leading to the conversion of high-quality agricultural land into residential or commercial zones. 
  - This leads to the diminishing availability of conductive land, limiting food production and drive farmers toward less fertile marginal land.
  - Malaysia's total land for food crops is shrinking as prime land is prioritised for real estate or industrial cash crops like oil palm. This competition creates land fragmentation, where smallholders operate on tiny, scattered plots that are inefficient to manage. 
  - This creates a major challenge for crucial systems like irrigation and drainage.
    - The small, disconnected plots characteristic makes it even more expensive and complex for government agencies to provide infrastructure. 
4. Climate Vulnerability and Lack of Financial Safety Nets
  - Increasingly unstable climate directly disrupts the Malaysian crop calendar. 
  - There is a lack of insurance framework that protects farmers from total loss from these climate disasters.
  - Massive floods in states like Johor and Pahang have wiped out thousands of hectares of vegetable and paddy.
    - Unlike the industrial sector, the agriculture sector does not have any comprehensive insurance system.
      - If a harvest is lost, the farmer loses their entire capital
      - This makes production non-conductive as farmers become risk-averse, sticking to traditional crops rather than experimenting with high-value crops that could boost their income and the nation's agriculture sector. 
  - Without a financial safety net to absorb climate shocks, the sector remains fragile and vulnerable to food security crises. 
5. Fragmented Value Chain and Dependence on Middlemen
  - Small-scale producers lack direct access to consumers and are forced to rely on a complex network of wholesalers.
  - Farmers receive only a small fraction of the final retail price, while high post-harvest losses occur due to poor logistics. 
  - Refrigerated transports or *cold chain* storage is underdeveloped in many rural areas.
    - This means that perishable goods like vegetables often spoil before reaching urban markets, leading to significant waste. 
  - Because of this, smallholders lack the volume to negotiate directly with supermarket. 
  - This creates a disincentive for conductive production, whereby even if a farmer works harder to produce more, the middlemen capture most of the profit, leaving the farmer without the surplus capital needed to improve their farm or support systems.

## Question 3

### Question A ⭐
1. Influence our export sectors
  - Both US and China are Malaysia's top export destinations. 
  - This means that despite having no direct immediate negative consequences on our economy, the trade-war still influenced our export sectors negatively.
  - Because of the trade war, the imported goods sold in both China and the US are more expensive.
  - This disincentivise both countries from continuing receiving exports at the same high volume from Malaysia.
  - As a result, the Malaysian's export sector took a hit in terms of profit
    - This forced production cuts, leading to layoffs
    - Additionally, because most of the jobs in Malaysia are tied to export activities
    - This led to a higher unemployment rate, and lesser money in circulation
2. Our E&E sector has seen positive investments
  - However, in specific sectors, positive growths in terms of investments can be observed
  - Because of the trade war, foreign companies involved in the technological field are looking towards cheaper sources of exported goods or parts.
  - For example, Micron is looking towards Southeast Asia to reduce their dependence on China and Taiwan.
  - Additionally, the CEO of NVIDIA, Jensen Huang, has also had multiple talks with the Malaysian Prime Minister about investments in Malaysia.
  - All of this is also possible because Malaysia is already ranked sixth as the world's semiconductor exporter and the nation's neutrality provided stability for foreign investors.
3. Exploited as China's trans-shipment hub
  - Malaysia has also been reprimanded for being China's trans-shipment hub to escape U.S. tariffs on China's goods
  - This places Malaysia at a bad position as it puts the country's neutral position, which is the country's main attraction, at risk
  - If no actions are taken to put a stop to this, the country risks exposure to secondary tariffs on top of the original and heavy scrutiny from the U.S.
  - This will negatively impact the country's GDP since the export sector one of the major contributor.
  - Fortunately, things have yet to escalate to heavy tariffs and scrutiny, instead, warnings and a call for collaboration to put a stop to the exploitation have taken place.
4. Currency and Financial Market Volatility
  - The trade tension between the U.S. and China had initially weakened the Malaysian Ringgit due to investors risk aversion
  - This means that foreign investors pulled out of the country, decreasing the total amount of foreign currency in Malaysia.
  - Although this made our exports more attractive as it has become cheaper, it caused a rise in the cost of living amongst the population.
  - Malaysian equity, especially export-linked equities, face volatility
    - This further exemplifies investors' and shareholders' risk aversion behaviour, causing even more to pull out.
5. National Semiconductor Strategy
  - The US China trade war gave Malaysia an extraordinary opportunity to further cement the nation's semiconductor position in the global stage.
  - With the National Semiconductor Strategy (NSS), the country aims to position itself as the leading designer of semiconductor
  - Historically, Malaysia's semiconductor manufacturing sector has always focused on "backend" tasks such as assembly, testing, and packaging rather than designing and innovating new designs.
  - With NSS, the government aims to create 60,000 highly skilled engineers by 2030 to propel itself as one of the global leaders in technology.
  - This will increase the employment rate as well as the profit gained from the semiconductor industry, increasing our GDP and boosting our economy.
