# Topic 1 - Industrialisation (Section A)

*What is industrialisation?*
- The process of converting a agriculture-based or resource-based economy to a manufacturing-based heavy society
	- Industrial revolution 
		- Build more factories
		- Mass production of items
- Resulting in higher total income and higher living standards
- A prominent example would be the industrial revolution in Britain in the 18th century, paving the way for modern industrialisation.

*What is de-industrialisation?*
- The shift from mass manufacturing to services

*Factors*
1. Increase in manufacturing productivity
    - This can be attributed to the invention of labour-saving technology
	  - This then reduces the amount of manual labour needed during the manufacturing process
	  - This leads to layoffs or less employment
	  - For example, in food production, packaging are done with machines rather than relying on manual efforts from humans
	  - This incentivise individuals to venture out of manufacturing into services.
2. Increase in overall population wealth
	- As the country becomes wealthier, more money is invested into other sectors in the country.
	- This exposes the population to other sectors and fields such as programming, medicine, and engineering. 
	- As the overall wealth increases, more people have the freedom to venture into other sectors and fields like programming, medicine, and engineering, all of which have higher upfront costs.
	- Additionally, investments into education serves as a catalyst that drives people to pursue careers outside of manufacturing
	- This reduces the volume of manpower going into manufacturing, forcing companies and governments to come up with creative solutions to sustain the manufacturing sector in Malaysia.
3. Globalisation and Offshoring
    - As global trade barriers fall, companies in developed or developing countries like Malaysia often move their manufacturing plants to countries with lower labour costs.
    - Multinational companies may find it cheaper to produce goods in countries like Vietnam or Indonesia due to lower minimum wages and less stringent regulations.
    - This leads to a shift where the home country keeps the high-value services such as design, marketing, and headquarters, but de-industrialise by closing down local physical factories.
    - Many Western and East Asian firms have moved their mass-production assembly lines out of their home countries to Southeast Asian hubs to maintain global price competitiveness.
        - It provides them with a much wider profit margin.
    - For example, NIKE, a global sports brand, manufactures most of its products in Vietnam, allowing them to earn more from selling while keeping manufacturing costs low.
4. Changing Patterns of Consume Demand
	- As society progresses, the way in which people spend their money changes.
	- Once people are able to meet their basic needs, their extra income is spent on services such as tourism, healthcare, luxury dining. Services are known as superior goods because the demand for them grows faster than income.
	- There is a limit to how many physical goods a person needs, but the demand for experiences and specialised services is almost limitless.
	- For example, in a middle-income country like Malaysia, as households get richer, they spend more on private education and international travel than on buying more household furniture, causing the service sector to grow while manufacturing stays flat. 
5. Outsourcing of in-house services
	- De-industrialisation can also be about how jobs are redefined rather than factories disappearing.
	- Initially, a large factory would have its own internal team of accountants, security guards, and cleaners.
		- Despite not being strictly manufacturing, these were still categorised under manufacturing roles.
	- Today, firms outsource these tasks to specialised service companies. The factory still produces the same amount of goods, but hundreds of jobs have moved from the "Manufacturing" sector to the "Service" sector.
	- For example, if a car manufacturer hires an external logistics firm to handle its shipping, the drivers are now "service workers" even though they are still part of the same industrial process.

# Topic 2 - Colonisation 

*What is the characteristics of Malaysia in the colonial period*

1. Rubber Plantation
	- Rubber was one of the most coveted raw materials during the colonial period due to its versatility and importance.
		- Rubber was used in cars, machinery, and industrialisation in Britain, and heavily used in wars for vehicles, weapons, and equipment.
	- Malaysia was one of the largest and most important producers in the world.
	- The plantation system was introduced by the British, relying heavily on labour forces imported from India and China
2. Mining Activities
	- Malaysia had a lot of raw earth materials that were valuable to the British Empire. 
	- For example, Perak, named after its vast reserve of tin, was the country's richest state. 
	- Malaysia became the world's leading tin producer in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, fuelling industrial growth in Britain
	- Mining also relied heavily on imported labour, particularly those imported from China. 
3. Robust Infrastructure
	- To handle the heavy export of rubber and tin, robust infrastructure had to be made
	- The British built railway system and roads near rubber plantations and mines to transport raw materials. 
	- These plantations and mining areas also had active trading hubs and these hubs also boomed with more stable and advanced infrastructures, creating communities that turned into cities that we occupy today. 
4. Raw Material Production
	- During the colonial period, the British were often involved in wars, leading to a high demand for rubber and tin ore.
	- Under British rule, Malaysia was developed with the sole purpose to feed London or Britain with raw materials. 
		- Malaysia was kept from developing its own manufacturing base at that time. 
	- Although the raw-materials were exported to Britain, it never generated any substantial wealth as no industry in Malaysia at the time could do anything with it.
	- Expensive imports had to be made from Britain. 
	- Because Malaysia's economy was extremely focused on just the two raw materials, Malaysia were extremely vulnerable to global events
		- During the Great Depression, the price of rubber and tin plummeted. Since the country lacked other sources to support the economy, it collapsed, leading to widespread poverty. 
5. Economic Dualism
	- The Chinese thrived as they were mostly hired to mine tin and became dominant in commerce and urban industries.
	- The Malay were largely confined to traditional roles as farmers, fishmongers, and fishermen, which limited their economic advancement compared to the Chinese. 
	- The Indians were primarily brought in by the British to work on rubber plantations. Many remained tied to estate labour, with low wages and limited upward mobility. 
	- This leads to a wide gap in socioeconomics between the communities
		- Chinese concentrated in mining and business
		- Malays remained in agriculture and fishing
		- Indians were locked into plantation work.
	- This imbalance became so serious that it fuelled ethnic tensions, culminating in racial riots, with the most notable and serious riots happening on the 13th of May 1969 

# Tutorial 3 and Tutorial 4 - Monetary and Fiscal Policies (Section A)

*What are the policies used during economic downturn and unbridled inflation?* 

Monetary policy and fiscal policy. Each of these policies can be subdivided into expansionary and contractionary policies. Expansionary monetary or fiscal policy is used during economic downturn to incentivise monetary spending amongst the population, encouraging economic growth. Contractionary monetary or fiscal policy is used during unhealthy inflation to maintain or strengthen the demand of the country's currency. 

*What is monetary policy?*

Monetary policy refers to the financial policies adopted by  the country's central bank to regulate the overall money supply, control liquidity, and influence interest rates. Its main goals are to maintain price stability, encourage sustainable economic growth, and manage inflation or deflation. 

During an economic downturn, the central bank typically adopts expansionary monetary policy. This involves several key measures:
1. Open Market Operations
	- The central bank buys government securities from commercial banks
	- This increases liquidity as the banks now holds larger cash reserves
	- This means that the banks can lend more money to businesses and consumers.
2. Commercial banks lowering loan interest rate 
	- This is to incentivise people to loan money from the bank, allowing money to flow in the economy.
	- People are encouraged to loan money to start their own business ventures, encouraging even more economic growth.
	- This is also controlled by the lowering of overnight policy rate (OPR), which is the benchmark for overnight lending between banks. 
3. Adjusting the required reserve policy (RRR)
	- This is the minimum percentage of deposits that commercial banks must hold as reserve. 
	- By lowering the percentage, banks are given more flexibility in lending money to businesses and consumers. 
	- This leads to more money supply and lower interest rates, stimulating spending and investments

Together these measures increase the money supply, lower interest rates, and stimulate demand, helping the economy recover from downturns.

> Contractionary policy is just the opposite of this

*What is fiscal policy?*

Fiscal policy is the financial strategies adopted by the governments to influence the economy through spending and taxation. Unlike monetary policy, fiscal policy is directly controlled by the government and focuses on how public funds are collected and used. 

Government spending influences job creation and infrastructure improvements, and overall demand in the economy, while taxation influences how much money households and businesses have available to spend or save. 

During an economic downturn, the government typically adopts expansionary fiscal policy, whereby the government spends more than they make. This involves measures such as:
1. Increasing government spending
	- The government spends more in improving existing systems and infrastructures
	- This leads to more jobs and money in circulation
	- Boosts demand for goods and services, encouraging business activity. 
2. Decreasing tax rates
	- Reduce the tax burden on households and businesses
	- Citizens have more disposable income to spend and save. 
	- Businesses retain more profits, encouraging investment and expansion. 

> Contractionary policy is just the opposite of this

# Topic 3 - Paddy Production/Agriculture

1. Aging Farmer Population and Labour Shortage
	- The agriculture sector is suffering labour drainage of youths, whereby youths are moving towards urban industrial sectors, leaving the average age of Malaysian farmers above 50 years old.
	- This creates a lack of human capital to drive modern agriculture initiatives, as older farmers are more likely to be resistant to digital changes.
	- Younger generations perceive agriculture as a dirty, dangerous, and difficult industry; it offers low prestige and unstable income compared to manufacturing or services. This results in an internal brain drain where most tech-savvy talent leaves rural areas.
	- Consequently, the sector suffers from a lack of innovation at the farm level, as young, talented workforce to manage drones, IoT sensors, or data analytics is lacking. 
	- This forces the industry to remain stuck in labour-intensive, low-yield traditional practices.
2. High Capital Costs and Slow Technology Adoption
	- Smallholders face a massive financial gap in adopting "smart farming" technologies like automated irrigation, robotics, and precision sensors.
	- The high cost makes production non-conductive for small-scale farmers who make up the majority of the food sector.
	- Modernising a farm requires significant upfront investment, which is often out of reach for small farmers. While government support systems exist, they are frequently criticised for being reactive rather than proactive. 
	- Because these farmers cannot afford the technology to increase their efficiency, they remain uncompetitive against cheaper imports from neighbouring countries like Thailand or Vietnam.
3. Competition for Arable Land and Urban Encroachment
	- Rapid urbanisation and the expansion of industrial estates are leading to the conversion of high-quality agricultural land into residential or commercial zones. 
	- This leads to the diminishing availability of conductive land, limiting food production and drive farmers toward less fertile marginal land.
	- Malaysia's total land for food crops is shrinking as prime land is prioritised for real estate or industrial cash crops like oil palm. This competition creates land fragmentation, where smallholders operate on tiny, scattered plots that are inefficient to manage. 
	- This creates a major challenge for crucial systems like irrigation and drainage.
		- The small, disconnected plots characteristic makes it even more expensive and complex for government agencies to provide infrastructure. 
4. Climate Vulnerability and Lack of Financial Safety Nets
	- Increasingly unstable climate directly disrupts the Malaysian crop calendar. 
	- There is a lack of insurance framework that protects farmers from total loss from these climate disasters.
	- Massive floods in states like Johor and Pahang have wiped out thousands of hectares of vegetable and paddy.
		- Unlike the industrial sector, the agriculture sector does not have any comprehensive insurance system.
		- If a harvest is lost, the farmer loses their entire capital
		- This makes production non-conductive as farmers become risk-averse, sticking to traditional crops rather than experimenting with high-value crops that could boost their income and the nation's agriculture sector. 
	- Without a financial safety net to absorb climate shocks, the sector remains fragile and vulnerable to food security crises. 
5. Fragmented Value Chain and Dependence on Middlemen
	- Small-scale producers lack direct access to consumers and are forced to rely on a complex network of wholesalers.
	- Farmers receive only a small fraction of the final retail price, while high post-harvest losses occur due to poor logistics. 
	- Refrigerated transports or *cold chain* storage is underdeveloped in many rural areas.
		- This means that perishable goods like vegetables often spoil before reaching urban markets, leading to significant waste. 
	- Because of this, smallholders lack the volume to negotiate directly with supermarket. 
	- This creates a disincentive for conductive production, whereby even if a farmer works harder to produce more, the middlemen capture most of the profit, leaving the farmer without the surplus capital needed to improve their farm or support systems.

## Asian Financial Crisis 

*What is the Asian Financial Crisis?*

It is a severe economic downturn that affected many Southeast Asian countries, including Malaysia. It was triggered by currency devaluations, capital flight, and financial instability.

The Philippines, Indonesia, Malaysia, South Korea, and Hong Kong were also affected by this crisis. However, China, Japan, Singapore, Brunei, and Vietnam were less affected.

*What Caused The Crisis in Malaysia*

1. Currency speculation and ringgit depreciation
	- The Malaysian ringgit dropped from 2.50 per US dollar to 4.80 per US dollar due to speculative attacks on the currency （According to Mahathir) .
	- This led to inflation, increased debt burdens, and reduced investor confidence.
	- This led to a direct hit on the Malaysian economy and caused a major rise in cost of living amongst Malaysian. 
2. Stock market crash
	- The Kuala Lumpur Stock Exchange (KLSE) Composite Index plunges from 1, 200 points to below 300 points
	- Many businesses and banks faced bankruptcy due to declining asset values
	- A lot of Malaysian investors also faced massive loss, leading to some declaring bankruptcy
3. High corporate debt and credit crunch
	- Many Malaysian companies had borrowed in USD, making repayments expensive as the ringgit weakened. 
	- Banks restricted lending, leading to a credit crisis and slowing economic activity.
4. Decline in foreign direct investment
	- Foreign investors pulled out funds, worsening the economic downturn. 
	- Investor confidence in Malaysia and the region plummeted. 

*So, how did Malaysia respond?*

1. Capital controls and currency peg
	- In an unorthodox manner, the government pegged the ringgit at 4.80 USD to stabilise the currency.
	- Restrictions were placed on the outflow of foreign capital to prevent speculative attacks.
2. Economic Stimulus and Bailouts
	- The government injected funds into troubled banks and businesses to prevent a financial collapse.
	- Infrastructure projects and domestic spending were increased to boost economic recovery. 
	- To revive the economy, in July 1998, the government announced a RM2b fiscal stimulus package, which worked out to about 1.5% of GDP at the time. 
3. Corporate and banking reforms
	- Stricter financial regulations were imposed to ensure stability in the banking system.
	- **Danaharta** and **Danamodal** agencies were created to manage and restructure bad loans
	- The banking system was further strengthened through mergers and acquisitions.
	- The Corporate Debt Restructuring Committee (CDRC) was formed as a platform for distressed corporates and lenders to work out feasible debt resolutions without resorting to legal proceedings. 
4. Avoided IMF bailout
	- Unlike Thailand and Indonesia, Malaysia refused IMF assistance
		- Implemented our own recovery strategy 
	- Allowed Malaysia to maintain economic sovereignty and avoid harsh IMF austerity measures. 

After Malaysia's success in overcoming the Asian Financial Crisis (AFC) without assistance from the IMF, the government implemented a series of long-term economic strategies to ensure stability and future growth.

These strategies focused on financial sector reforms, economic diversification, and industrial modernisation. 

*So, what did the government do specifically?*

1. Strengthening the nation's financial and banking sector
	- Danaharta and Danamodal initiatives
		- Danaharta is a government agency created to buy and restructure bad loans from banks
		- Danamodal is a fund set up to recapitalise struggling banks and restore financial stability
	- Stricter banking regulations
		- The government reduced the number of banks by merging banks to strengthen financial institutions
			- The government focused on creating anchor banks that can withstand economic downturns or financial crisis
		- Reformation of the corporate governance to enforce transparency and accountability in banking and business sectors
	- Liberalisation of the financial market
		- The capital market was gradually opened, attracting foreign investors while maintaining economic independence. 
		- The Ringgit peg was eventually removed, converting to a floating system
2. Economic Diversification and Industrial Growth
	- Malaysia shifted from Agriculture to Manufacturing and Services
		- The manufacturing sector overtook agriculture as the primary GDP contributor.
		- Industries such as E&E, automotive, and palm oil grew.
	- Malaysia became export centric
		- Focus is placed on high-value exports, including semiconductors, electrical appliances, and petrochemicals.
		- Incentives for Foreign Direct Investment (FDI) in high-tech industries. 
	- Development of the services sector
		- Expansion in finance, tourism, and Islamic banking to reduce reliance on commodities. 
3. Mega Infrastructure Projects
	- Stimulating growth through public investments
		- This boosts employment and domestic demand
			- KLIA become a major regional aviation hub
			- Putrajaya is made to decentralise government operations
			- LRT and MRT systems were made to improve urban transportation
	- Focused on Technology and Digital Economy Expansion
		- Launched the Multimedia Super Corridor (MSC) Malaysia to promote tech start-ups.
			- Cyberjaya was development as Malaysia's Silicon Valley
4. Encouraging Private Sector and Entrepreneurship
	- Small and Medium Enterprise Development
		- SMEs where provided with tax incentives and grants
		- SME Corp and MARTRADE were created to help Malaysian businesses expand globally. 
	- Islamic Banking and Finance Growth

## US-China Trade War

*How does it affect Malaysian economy?*

**How it risks our export sector**

Both China and the US has always been Malaysia's top trading partners. Because of this, although there are no direct consequences towards Malaysia, it affects our global export demand and appeal. With the trade war, the trade between the two countries will reduce as the prices of goods and services or raw materials exported are more expensive. 

Malaysia does not only sell finished products to the U.S and China, but also parts that are used to build products in respective countries. The flow of the exports are usually as follow:

$$\text{Malaysia} \to \text{China (assembly)} \to \text{USA (Consumer)}$$

Due to the trade war of between U.S and China, the manufacturing process in China and the products sold in the U.S. may become more expensive, harming both consumers and manufacturers. This places manufacturers in a bad position as it is harder for them to sell their products and make profit. As a result, they begin to cut production, and subsequently buys less from Malaysia. Consequently, this leads to an overall reduction in the profits made in export in Malaysia. 

Since Malaysia is a very export-centric country, it would heavily impact our GDP. Most of the employments in Malaysia are linked with export-related activities. Lower demand in our exported products will influence our labour force and investments. This creates a domino effect whereby lower demand leads to lower profit, which then leads to production cuts and layoffs. As a result, the unemployment rate rises and the economy suffers as a whole. 

Thus, although the U.S. - China trade war does not have direct, immediate impact on Malaysia, because these two countries are our top trading partners, any trade policies that harm trade between China and U.S. will influence our export sector.
